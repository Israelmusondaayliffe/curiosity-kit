# Discovery and offer

Use when a founder needs to learn from customers, choose a reachable first offer, prepare a pilot or examine pricing. Produce the requested decision, questionnaire or complete offer. Do not turn planning into building a product, contacting a customer or accepting terms.

## Ask for the information that changes the decision

Use supplied context and accessible facts. Separate three kinds of unknown:

- A fact that can be read from existing evidence.
- A founder choice, such as capacity, intended business or acceptable risk.
- A customer fact, such as the last workaround, budget authority or willingness to commit.

Do not ask the founder to guess the third kind. Draft questions for the actual person or name the evidence needed. Resolve prerequisites before dependent questions: buyer and job before offer promise; deliverable/capacity before price; chosen objective before channel. Independent questions can share a short round. “Unknown” is a valid answer. A founder agreeing with the assistant is not market validation or permission to send.

Select stage-sensitive questions, not a mandatory interview script:

| Situation | Most useful questions |
|---|---|
| Before a product or paid work | What concrete behavior shows the problem? What happens today? Who bears the consequence? |
| Users but weak revenue | What is the current workaround? What smallest useful outcome could they commit to? What happens when they use it without help? |
| Paying customers | What do they keep buying or using? Which narrow expansion follows observed value? What change in their world affects future fit? |
| Internal service | Who sponsors it, who uses it, and would it survive without its champion? |

Costly action, actual usage, payment and repeat use are stronger commercial evidence than compliments, but weaker signals still help identify language and questions. Do not apply a universal interview-count or survey-percentage threshold. No workaround found is a counter-signal to investigate, not proof that a new category cannot exist.

## Prepare or interpret discovery

Ask about specific past events before hypothetical enthusiasm. Useful prompts include:

- “Walk me through the last time you did this.”
- “What happened next? What did you try instead?”
- “How often has that happened in the period you described?”
- “What time, cost or consequence did it create?”
- “Who chose the current approach, and who can approve changing it?”

Do not assume something stalled, failed or was expensive unless the source says so. Ask whether it did. Avoid “Would you love a tool that saves hours?” or hiding a pitch inside supposedly neutral research. Separate the person's words from your interpretation. Record contradictions, not just evidence favoring the founder's idea.

When a questionnaire is requested, make it usable by its recipient: purpose, relevant known context, the decisions the answers will inform, prioritised questions with one idea each, blank answer space and permission to skip or say “I don't know.” Include deadlines or effort estimates only when supplied or explicitly proposed as such. A questionnaire for one expert does not become a population survey or get sent automatically.

After an actual response, distinguish what happened, what was inferred, what remains unknown and what evidence would change the decision. Do not invent quotes, motives or answers. Observing unaided usage can reveal confusion or an unexpected job; a model role-playing a buyer cannot supply that evidence.

## Compare actual alternatives and choose a small offer

Ask what the buyer would do without the offer. Include manual work, an internal hire, an existing tool, doing nothing or deferring the job when evidenced. A famous competitor is not necessarily in the customer's consideration set. Compare the alternatives on the buyer's job, effort, switching costs, outcome, trust, capacity and known cost basis. Keep missing information visible.

If the choice is open, compare genuinely different ways to deliver value, such as a concierge service, a narrow tool or a change inside the current workflow. Cosmetic headline variants do not test a different offer. If evidence already settles the choice, do not manufacture alternatives.

Define a small complete offer that can be delivered and evaluated:

- Who it serves, in which situation, and the job it addresses.
- The specific output or service, delivery method and time boundary.
- Inclusions, exclusions and what the buyer must provide.
- The first useful result and observable acceptance criteria.
- Founder time/capacity, necessary tools, variable costs and major dependencies.
- Proposed payment basis, price/currency and applicable terms that still need approval.
- Available proof, material limitations and the next disconfirming check.

Do not replace proof with bonuses, scarcity, guarantees or inflated return-on-investment claims. A proposed “90-minute workflow audit and written checklist” is a deliverable. “Save every agency ten hours” requires evidence that supports that population and causal claim. A pilot outcome may be a concrete artifact or measured improvement; state which before evaluating it.

## Examine pricing without pretending certainty

Treat pricing as a commercial decision informed by evidence, not a universal formula. Separate:

- The founder's proposed price.
- A competitor's advertised price, with scope/date/currency and relevant billing basis.
- What the customer says they might pay.
- A specific approved purchase commitment.
- An actual payment and any refund/recurrence.

Use a small set of relevant price/package alternatives. Compare customer value evidence, scope, delivery costs, capacity, frequency and the cost of alternatives. Survey willingness to pay is stated preference; it cannot certify paid conversion. Do not import universal $10/$100/$1,000 starting prices, automatically add a guarantee or assert an optimal price from a tiny sample.

For supplied numbers, show units and assumptions. Example, explicitly synthetic: a $300 pilot requiring four delivery hours and $40 of variable tool costs leaves $260 before overhead, tax and other costs, or $65 per delivery hour before those costs. That is contribution arithmetic under stated assumptions, not net profit or proof of market demand. If preparation/sales time is excluded, say so. A delivery cap is not a claim that the founder can sell every available slot.

If customers differ materially, separate segments/packages rather than average unlike buyers. When budget, scope or delivery cost is unknown, give the comparison that is possible and name the missing input. Do not fill it with a plausible number. Real discounts, guarantees, contracts, payment links and billing changes need the user's requested terms and the actual action's permissions.

## Design one informative next check

Name the uncertain decision, hypothesis, observable evidence, eligible participants, bounded artifact or action, observation window, owner and interpretation. A sample report may test comprehension; a paid pilot may test willingness to commit; repeat use may test continued value. Keep those outcomes distinct.

Use the smallest useful test. A venture-scale launch is not required for a small profitable service. A prototype needs a decision it can answer and is not production-ready by default. For real contact or transactions, stop at the action boundary unless separately authorized. Keep a short decision note only when future work depends on it: evidence, choice, unresolved assumption and next check. No tracker or new orchestration system is needed.

Finish with [the claim review](../references/evidence-and-review.md). Deliver the complete requested offer/questionnaire/decision, not just instructions for the user to write it. Preserve an honest “not enough evidence to choose” when that is the result.
