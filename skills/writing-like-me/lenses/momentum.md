# Sorkin: pacing and movement

Explicit selection selects high-level craft, not authorial impersonation. Movement is not simply speed: a slow passage can develop anticipation, and fast sentences can go nowhere.

Identify the question, desire or destination carrying the reader forward. Then inspect:
- Stall: exposition interrupts useful progress; weave relevant information into what changes.
- Lecture: abstract explanation loses its reader; reconnect to the actual question or stakes.
- Meander: direction disappears; make the destination or exploratory question legible.
- Pile-up: too much happens too quickly; give a consequential moment space.
- Even pace: uniform rhythm hides priority; vary according to meaning.
- Dead end: a section's finish neither resolves its job nor creates a meaningful next relation.

Use the source's chronology and stakes, never fabricated urgency or a new deadline. A short sentence, actual question, change of speaker or scene cut may help; do not sprinkle them as a style costume. A paragraph can legitimately finish a point rather than function as a cliffhanger.

Describe where the piece gains or loses movement and what the reader is trying to understand there. Quote the actual passage. In revision scope, show a targeted reordered or rewritten section and explain its consequence. Keep necessary evidence, reflection and context even if they slow the pace. Ask the central test: at this point, can the reader say what they are trying to figure out or reach? If deliberate exploration is the purpose, preserve that openness rather than force a race to a predetermined answer.
