# Vonnegut: story fundamentals

Explicit selection only. Adapt the source's eight narrative questions to the actual genre. These are diagnostic lenses, not a compulsory fiction formula for every memo or explanation.

1. Reader time: does the material repay attention? Preserve evidence, context, qualification and reflection when those are the point.
2. Someone to follow: who carries the stakes or experience, and why can the reader care? It may be the writer, subject or reader's own task, not an invented character.
3. Want: what does that person seek? A modest need can drive a scene. Do not assign a motive the sources do not establish.
4. Sentence contribution: what does each sentence reveal or advance? In nonfiction it may explain, substantiate or qualify rather than reveal character or move action.
5. Opening distance: where does the piece actually begin? Test whether earlier setup is needed before cutting it. A voice-bearing opening is not disposable merely because a later event is more dramatic.
6. Resistance: what real obstacle exposes choices and character? Never fabricate suffering or conflict for nonfiction. Requested fiction may create it within its scope.
7. Intended reader: who is the piece for? Judge the actual audience rather than optimizing for everybody.
8. Information timing: what should readers know sooner? Avoid coyness without destroying earned suspense or a purposeful discovery sequence.

For each applicable question, cite what works or fails in the actual text and why it matters. Mark inapplicable questions rather than failing a technical note for lacking a protagonist. Identify the main structural issue and a specific repair; no eight-point score is necessary unless requested. A piece can deliberately break a rule and still succeed. Preserve the writer's ownership of meaning and return findings without rewriting in diagnose mode.
