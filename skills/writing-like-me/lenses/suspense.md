# Hitchcock: earned suspense

Explicit selection only. Distinguish a sudden surprise from suspense created by a reader knowing a real risk before a character does. Identify what the reader knows, what the subject knows, what is wanted and what could actually be lost. Not every explanation or tutorial benefits from suspense.

Inspect the source-supported opportunities:
- Plant: establish the meaningful risk early enough for later events to carry tension.
- Dramatic irony: let the reader recognize a discrepancy the subject has not yet recognized.
- Clock: use an actual deadline or observed passage of time, never invented urgency.
- Gap: reveal the distance between a desire and its obstacle.
- Delay: slow a consequential moment when anticipation earns the attention.
- False relief: use an actual apparent resolution followed by complication; do not invent events.
- Zoom: a supplied concrete detail makes a high-stakes moment legible.

Locate buried stakes, prematurely resolved tension, uniform pace and a missing information relationship. Separate deliberate mystery from accidental confusion. A reader who lacks basic orientation cannot feel the intended suspense.

Return the current effect and a few passage-specific opportunities, identifying the change in information or timing and why it serves the piece. Revise a sample only when asked. For nonfiction, a missing scene/detail stays missing; for explicitly requested fiction, invention remains within the fiction's scope. Ask whether an intended change alters the writer's position or history rather than deciding it. Earned tension is preferable to manipulative withholding. No routine hook, cliffhanger or countdown is required.
