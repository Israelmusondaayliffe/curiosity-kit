# Clear writing and editing for guides

Adapted from the complete Pstack Unslop method and relevant technical-writing principles. Stable source rule IDs below retain traceability; gaps belong to the source numbering. This is a reader-centered editing method, not a test of whether text was written by AI.

## Apply the method

Read the whole passage and identify its job before changing words. Mark unclear claims, missing actors, unnecessary abstraction, filler and formatting that obscures the task. Rewrite for the intended adult reader, preserving meaning, tone, requirements, necessary uncertainty and supported specificity. Then reread the passage in its full reader path, not merely as isolated sentences.

Protect exact code, command strings, filenames, quotations, titles and technical terms whose precision matters. A familiar word can still be wrong if it erases a distinction. Preserve a deliberate user voice when it serves the guide. Do not invent facts or measurements to make prose sound concrete. If evidence is absent, state the bounded uncertainty or remove the unsupported claim.

## Complete adaptation of the Unslop catalog

- **3, superficial participial claims.** Clauses such as “ensuring success” can imply an unsupported outcome. Name the actual action and its evidence, or cut the clause. A real process relationship can remain when explained.
- **5, vague attribution.** Replace “experts say” with the relevant identified source and claim, or remove it. A source's authority must match the subject and date.
- **7, inflated vocabulary.** Prefer familiar literal words over decorative abstractions. Keep a specialist term when it is accurate and needed, then explain it locally.
- **8, indirect copulas.** Use “is” or “has” when “serves as” or “stands as” adds no meaning. Preserve a real functional relationship when it matters.
- **9, formulaic contrast.** State the useful point directly instead of reflexive “not just… but…” framing. Retain a comparison when the reader genuinely needs to distinguish alternatives.
- **10, forced threes.** Let the material determine how many items belong together. Do not invent a third benefit or remove a fourth prerequisite for rhythm.
- **11, synonym cycling.** Give a thing a stable name. Alternating “brief,” “specification” and “request” can suggest different objects; use separate terms only for separate concepts.
- **12, false ranges.** Use “from… to…” for a meaningful span. Otherwise list the actual topics or choices without implying a progression.
- **13, distracting punctuation.** Split overloaded asides and dash chains into clear sentences. Follow the user's or project's stated punctuation rules in authored prose, such as a ban on em dashes. Without one, the source's categorical bans are softened: use conventional punctuation when it clarifies meaning. Always preserve exact syntax and quotations.
- **14, colon clutter.** A colon can introduce a list or example. Do not use it to prop up a sentence whose relationship is unclear; rewrite that relationship explicitly.
- **15, excessive bold.** Reserve emphasis for a meaningful choice, warning or navigation cue. If every noun is emphasized, none helps the reader.
- **16, redundant inline labels.** Remove a label that merely repeats the following words. Keep a short informative lead-in when it aids scanning and the rest supplies new detail.
- **17, heading capitalization.** Prefer sentence case for guide headings unless the user's established style or a quoted product title requires otherwise.
- **18, decorative emoji.** Remove visual decoration that distracts or adds no meaning. If an icon carries meaning, pair it with text and an accessible equivalent.
- **19, quotation consistency.** Keep quotation marks consistent in prose, following any stated user or project preference, and preserve exact quotes. Use safe literal syntax in copyable code/prompts; do not change smart/literal characters blindly across technical content.
- **20, chatbot residue.** Remove conversational service closings and response announcements from the artifact. The reader needs the guide, not a record of its generation.
- **22, flattery.** Replace reflexive praise and exaggerated agreement with the relevant answer or action. Respectful reassurance may help a learner recover, but should not certify a wrong answer.
- **23, filler.** Cut phrases that delay the point without qualifying it. “To” often replaces “in order to”; preserve words that change scope or conditions.
- **24, stacked hedging.** Reduce several weak qualifiers to one accurate uncertainty statement. Do not turn “not yet tested” into certainty merely to sound confident.
- **25, generic conclusions.** End with the actual finish, next decision or useful reference. Avoid empty promises about transformation or the future.
- **26, abstract metaphor nouns.** Name the concrete mechanism, object or action instead of a fashionable metaphor. If an analogy is useful for teaching, explain its mapping and boundary using the adult explanation method.
- **27, mechanism over mood.** State what happens, what the reader does or what evidence shows. “The checker reports unresolved source IDs” is useful; “confidence at your fingertips” is not. A measured number is appropriate only when supported. Cut sentences that could fit any unrelated guide unchanged and add no task-specific value.
- **28, sentence load.** Split a sentence when the reader must backtrack to find its actor, action or condition. One main idea per sentence is a useful default, not a reason to detach a necessary qualifier.
- **29, actors and voice.** Name who performs an action when the reader needs to know. Passive voice is reasonable when the actor is unknown or irrelevant; never invent an actor just to satisfy a style rule.
- **30, weak verbs and adverbs.** Choose the verb that describes the action. Replace unsupported intensifiers with evidence or remove them. Do not replace “quickly” with an invented time.
- **31, plain words.** Use a familiar word when it means the same thing. Retain precise domain language where a simpler substitute changes the fact.
- **32, mannered prose.** Remove stock aphorisms, theatrical fragments and personified systems when a literal explanation would be clearer. An intentional memorable example can remain if it teaches rather than decorates.
- **33, over-compression.** Restore articles, verbs and relationships lost in shorthand. A guide should not make readers decode arrows, unexplained abbreviations or fragments merely to understand an instruction.

## Guide-specific editing passes

First repair meaning: unsupported claims, unclear conditions, inconsistent object names and missing reasons. Then repair sentence flow and paragraph order. Finally tune formatting and surface style. This order prevents a polished sentence from hiding a broken procedure.

Read each instruction as a reader about to act. Can they identify the input, actor, action and expected result? Are a warning and its relevant condition available before the action? Does the explanation supply the concept required at that point, rather than forcing a glossary detour?

Compare obligations by meaning within clauses, bullets and sections. Keep one owning statement where two sentences demand the same thing. Preserve distinct conditions, exceptions, output facts and useful teaching repetition. A worked example, a later unaided attempt and a brief recap can repeat an idea for different purposes.

Finish by reading aloud or simulating a cold read of the prose. Do not call that simulation a real learner test. Verify that editing did not alter sources, numerical constraints, quotation meaning, uncertainty or exact prompt/code strings. When brevity and comprehension conflict, keep the words that let the reader act correctly.
