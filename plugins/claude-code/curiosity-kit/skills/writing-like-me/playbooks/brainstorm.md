# Brainstorm

Use when the writer wants to explore possible material and has not selected a piece. Begin with what they have noticed, experienced, questioned or been unable to resolve. Do not open with a framework questionnaire.

Listen for surprise, contradiction, changed understanding, friction, cross-domain patterns and experience-earned insight. Ask a natural follow-up tied to the actual answer: what happened, what differed from expectation, what changed, what remains confusing, what they see that a reader might miss. Do not supply a lesson or imply that enthusiasm proves significance.

For factual or persuasive material, use New, True and Useful as questions:
- New: does it make something unfamiliar accessible, or show a fresh angle on familiar ground? Familiar explanations are still valuable.
- True: is the claim factually supported, logically defensible or honest to the writer's actual experience? A feeling can be authentic without establishing a general fact.
- Useful: might it generate thought, reframe a problem, clarify an idea or enable action? These are possible benefits, not mandatory boxes.

Reflect promising material in the writer's language without upgrading tentative observations into claims. Offer distinct candidate directions only when requested; mark them as proposed frames and tie each to supplied evidence. Do not insist on conflict, novelty, transformation or an actionable takeaway for a contemplative piece.

Stop when the writer selects a direction or asks to move on. If energy and specificity suggest a direction, offer a brief choice rather than assuming agreement. Within an already authorized complete-writing task, proceed once enough material exists; don't create an unnecessary stage approval. Carry unresolved questions into [interview](interview.md) or [outline](outline.md). Return the useful ideas, source cues and the next unresolved choice, not a fictional finished argument.
