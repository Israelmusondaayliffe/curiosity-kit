# Focused craft: hook, thesis, promise, analogy, transition

Select only the requested operation and craft. Preserve diagnose/propose/revise scope, the writer's actual position and source evidence. A craft call never starts reviewers or a new writing lifecycle. Respect the requested number of options. When unspecified, give one strong hook/transition or a small genuinely useful choice set; do not force three because a template once showed three. Examples of moves are not permission to invent experience, data or events.

## Hook

Find what the piece actually delivers, what matters to the reader and which supplied material can open it honestly. A hook earns attention; it need not be the thesis, and a vivid opening can still foreground the wrong idea. Use [BLUF](bluf.md) for that distinction.

The ten source hook families remain available:
- Already-happened: open on a supported outcome before explaining how it happened.
- Paradigm flip: challenge an assumed truth with evidence, not contrarian performance.
- Visceral moment: a sourced sensory detail or emotion makes an experience tangible.
- Time stamp: a real date or moment establishes the change or stakes.
- Failed expert: a documented competent person's mistake exposes a useful limit.
- Future glimpse: a clearly framed projection grounded in observable change, not a future fact.
- Contradiction: hold two supported but apparently conflicting observations together.
- Vulnerability: use an admission the writer actually supplied, not manufactured intimacy.
- Stats shock: use a sourced number whose comparison matters; never invent its denominator or significance.
- Definitional challenge: clarify a familiar term by showing what its usual definition misses.

Choose families that fit the content and emotional register. Write a usable opening, usually one to three sentences; label alternative approaches only when helpful. Remove throat-clearing without deleting necessary orientation. Check that the body repays the hook.

## Thesis

Identify what the writer is saying or exploring before making it memorable. Candidate approaches include reframing familiar material, connecting domains, naming an observed but poorly described phenomenon, exposing a supported mechanism, a useful paradox, a genuine question, pattern recognition, a qualified synthesis, a clarifying distinction or a grounded prediction. Novelty is not required and coined jargon is not a substitute for thought.

Test each proposed thesis: is it specific enough to mean something, supported at its stated scope, arguable when the genre calls for argument, and generative rather than a slogan? Distinguish a claim the writer made from your proposed stronger version. Do not promote an attractive latent argument without writer judgment when it changes their position. Return one or two sentences per option and the relevant source cue when needed.

## Promise

Determine the real reader payoff and whether the piece can deliver it. A promise may preview a destination, framework, question, challenge, discovery or map. An admission of what learning cost the writer requires source support. Possible functions are appetite, stakes, expectations, trust and momentum; none demands inflated urgency or a transformation claim.

Use concrete value, source-earned authority and appropriate emotional register. Ask whether the reader would be satisfied if the body delivered exactly this promise. Match the energy of what follows. Do not promise resolution the writer has not reached, fabricate scarcity or turn an informative article into a sales pitch. Return the requested options as usable prose with a short distinction between approaches.

## Analogy

Identify the unfamiliar mechanism, then what this particular audience already understands. Match underlying relationships rather than shared adjectives. Physical processes, ordinary activities and social situations can offer useful vehicles, but a vehicle requiring equal explanation is no help.

For each option state the correspondence, what it clarifies and where it breaks. Test whether the mapping predicts something useful and whether it introduces a false causal implication. Prefer concrete and unfamiliar-to-familiar explanations; avoid mixing metaphors. Keep literal precision beside the image for technical work. Never imply that an analogy is evidence. If none holds, explain the mechanism directly rather than force one.

## Transition

Read the end of section A and start of B in the whole piece. Identify the actual relationship, then choose:
- Logical bridge: B follows from, qualifies, challenges or applies A.
- Story flow: an event, image, question or continuing action carries the reader forward.
- Sudden shift: a purposeful contrast or cut whose connection becomes clear soon enough.

Arguments and how-to work usually benefit from logical bridges; personal essays and profiles may favor story flow or an earned cut. These are weights, not rules. A bridge is usually one or two sentences, or no added sentence when juxtaposition already works. Do not fabricate a relationship to smooth a discontinuity; flag the underlying gap. Keep useful formal connectors, cut mechanical “moreover”/“another point” only when they add nothing. Vary the move when repeated transitions make the structure audible.
