# Analyze claims and write the complete artifact

Use this method when researched or supplied evidence must become a claim assessment, comparison, report, memo or recommendation. Start from the actual brief and evidence, not a polished conclusion in search of citations. A claim ledger alone is not the final report unless the user asked for a ledger.

## Check readiness

Confirm the reader, requested decision or use, source boundaries, supplied template, format and length. Inspect the relevant source record from [research](research.md). Missing evidence may narrow what can be asserted, but it does not require abandoning every useful section. Name the affected gap; do not fill it with plausible facts.

Preserve raw sources and user-supplied text as evidence. Put your interpretation or rewrite in a separate working section or artifact. Before editing an existing draft, reread its current contents so intervening user changes survive.

## Test the claims that carry the result

Use one specific claim at a time. Split a sentence when its clauses need different evidence. “The program increased satisfaction and productivity across the company” may contain three unsupported expansions: causation, a second outcome and a larger population.

For each material claim:

1. State the exact claim and intended scope.
2. Define its support test: direct statement or measurement, matching population/context/date, baseline or comparator, definitions and freshness.
3. Read the actual supporting passage or data and record its location. Do not rely only on a snippet or a worker's account of it.
4. Search or inspect the plausible disconfirmation. Record contradictions and important missing data.
5. Assign the status below and the wording it permits.
6. Give confidence a qualitative reason based on directness, source quality, independence, consistency and freshness. Do not turn subjective confidence into an invented percentage.
7. Note any calculation, assumption or other claim on which it depends and where it appears in the artifact. Keep this compact; a full graph is unnecessary for a short answer.

| Status | Meaning | Permitted draft treatment |
|---|---|---|
| Supported | Read evidence directly supports this scoped claim | State it with nearby support and the source's actual limitations. |
| Inferred | Supported premises require an analytical step | State the inference and its assumptions, not a source quotation or proven fact. |
| Disputed | Credible relevant evidence remains inconsistent | Show the material disagreement and why it remains unresolved. Conflicting passages can occur within one source. |
| Unresolved | Evidence is missing, inaccessible or inadequate | Remove, narrow or explicitly present it as an open question. Do not write it as settled. |

Confidence and status do different jobs. High confidence in a transcription does not prove that the source's causal claim is sound. A source's reported confidence interval is a sourced statistic; your confidence in a recommendation is judgment. Preserve that distinction.

### Numerical and causal claims

Recompute arithmetic with an appropriate available tool. Retain numerator, denominator, unit, period, population, exclusions and rounding. Check that compared values use compatible definitions and conditions. Distinguish percentage points from percentage change; totals from unique entities; quoted price from payment; respondents from all participants; publication date from event date.

A change after an intervention does not by itself establish that the intervention caused it. Compare the design, baseline, controls, confounders and source's own claim. If they cannot support causation, describe the observed association or sequence and keep the explanation uncertain.

Synthetic illustration: eight of ten survey respondents favored a change, from twenty people invited. The defensible statement is “8 of 10 respondents favored it.” It is not “80% of everyone invited favored it,” and it does not establish what nonrespondents thought. If an owning correction changes the count, update every derived figure and conclusion that depended on it.

### Recommendations

A recommendation needs:

- The decision it serves and relevant approved constraints.
- Supported factual premises.
- Assumptions connecting those premises to the proposed action.
- Credible alternatives, including delay or no action when relevant.
- Known cost, risk, reversibility and missing evidence.
- A condition that would change the recommendation.

State the recommendation as your judgment. Do not disguise it as a fact proved by the bibliography. Do not invent the user's preference or call a recommended purchase approved. If different preferences would select different options, show the conditional choice or ask only the unresolved material preference.

## Write backwards from the reader's need

Choose a structure that serves the decision rather than the order of source discovery. A useful decision memo leads with the answer, then decisive evidence, reasoning and alternatives, limits and next action. A comparison can organize by the user's criteria. A requested template governs even when another shape seems elegant.

For a substantial document, make a short section map: each required section's job, the assessed claims it will use and what the reader must already understand. Introduce a concept before relying on it later. Removing jargon does not fix an unexplained prerequisite.

Write the whole requested piece. Fill each required section with actual analysis, examples or actions at useful depth. Do not stop at an outline, opening options, a list of sources or one exemplary paragraph when a complete artifact was requested. If an essential section is blocked, provide the usable sections and identify the precise missing input and its consequence. Do not disguise blanks as a finished deliverable.

Use the forms that communicate best: prose for reasoning, a list for actions, a table for a meaningful comparison, an actual example for a difficult distinction. Do not force a format merely because a donor uses it. An example must be sourced or clearly labeled synthetic; it cannot introduce invented user facts or business results.

Interactive fragment or beat-by-beat work is optional when the user selects that mode. In that case, keep source fragments separate, offer reachable next moves and write the selected move, rereading between edits. A complete-draft request should not be slowed into paragraph approvals.

## Preserve fidelity while making it readable

Draft facts from supported claims with citations close to the precise sentence they support. Prefer the owning source and an inspectable passage. One citation at the end of a paragraph cannot support several unrelated assertions. Include additional citations only when each adds relevant support, rather than the appearance of consensus.

Label analysis naturally: “I infer,” “This suggests,” or an equally clear formulation. Avoid stacking hedges; state what is known and the single important uncertainty. When evidence is disputed, show the disagreement at the affected conclusion, not only in a disclaimer at the end.

Check claims introduced by editing:

- A transition such as “therefore” may create a new causal or logical assertion.
- “All,” “always,” “proven” or “will” may exceed the source's scope.
- A headline, chart caption, announcement or summary may drop a condition retained in the body.
- A merged quotation may change meaning even if each fragment is exact.
- A polished recommendation may imply approval, a service promise or ongoing work that never occurred.

Keep quotations within applicable limits, preserve their meaning and attribute them accurately. A paraphrase must preserve the same conditions and uncertainty. Never use a secondary summary as though you directly inspected the primary source.

Write plainly and specifically. Remove inflated claims, filler and generic conclusions. Preserve terms when changing them would alter the source's definition; explain the term instead. Use supplied voice evidence when relevant, but do not invent opinions to make the prose sound personal. Optional writing or domain helpers improve a complete local draft; they are not required to produce one.

## Complete alternatives without incoherent blending

When the task requires alternatives, make each a usable complete option with its assumptions and evidence. Compare them against the same user criteria. Choose one base if synthesis is warranted, carry over specific compatible strengths and name material tradeoffs. Recheck the integrated argument. If the evidence supports incompatible choices under different assumptions, preserve that conditional decision instead of combining them into one impossible recommendation.

Do not generate extra alternatives merely to fill a quota. A small claim audit or a clear factual memo often needs one direct result.

## Inspect before review

Read the artifact through five lenses:

1. **Brief:** all questions, sections, constraints and requested outputs are fulfilled.
2. **Evidence:** final factual wording stays within support; citations and figures match actual sources.
3. **Reasoning:** assumptions, alternatives, causal limits and reconsideration conditions are visible.
4. **Reader:** concepts arrive before use and the intended decision/action is possible.
5. **Language:** prose is clear and complete without invented promises, certainty or voice.

When a file deliverable requires a traceable evidence record, keep it in the [evidence contract](../references/evidence-contract.md) format and run its checker on the final artifact. A checker finding can reveal a broken link between declared IDs; a passing record cannot establish that your final prose is true or complete. Review the actual draft using [review and repair](review-and-repair.md), then perform final [verification and delivery](verify-and-deliver.md).
