# Assumption Challenger: working bias taxonomy

Use in planning/challenge and again in verification. Apply [shared evidence definitions](../evidence-and-choice.md). These are hypotheses about observable reasoning patterns, not diagnoses of the user, inferred motives or proof that a conclusion is false. A correct decision can have imperfect reasoning; an unusual decision can be well supported. State the pattern, relevant evidence, competing explanation and a useful corrective test.

## Input framing

| Pattern | What to inspect | Corrective test and caution |
|---|---|---|
| Confirmation bias | Supporting examples without comparable contrary cases; all sources share a viewpoint; the question presupposes the preferred answer; objections dismissed without engagement. | Seek the strongest counterposition and ask what could change the conclusion. A short brief may omit work already done; inspect supplied evidence before claiming it was ignored. |
| Anchoring | An unexplained price, timeline, benchmark, framework or single precedent dominates the choice. | Compare a relevant range and constraints, or derive the figure from inputs. An anchor can be a genuine budget/deadline; do not replace a user constraint with a supposed market norm. |
| Survivorship/selection bias | Success stories without failed, abandoned or excluded cases; imitation of famous outcomes; a sample selected by outcomes. | Inspect who entered and who disappeared from the sample. Seek a denominator and comparable failures. Do not invent a base rate if the denominator is unavailable. Selection mechanisms can also create an overly pessimistic failure sample. |
| Availability | A vivid incident or recent event dominates attention while common, less dramatic operational factors disappear. | Compare frequency, consequence and relevance using actual evidence. A recent incident may be an informative regime change, not merely salience. |
| Sunk cost | Prior effort is used as the main reason to continue despite changed prospective value; pivoting is equated with personal failure. | Compare future costs/benefits from today's position. Ask what would be chosen without the irrecoverable expenditure, while preserving real switching costs, reusable assets and commitments. |
| Planning fallacy | Best-case productivity, missing dependencies, no contingency or outside-view comparison; all resources assumed continuously available. | Use relevant completed work, dependencies and capacity evidence to test estimates. Do not import a generic delay percentage or assert a deadline is impossible without evidence. |
| Optimism bias | Specific success scenario alongside vague failure conditions; “when” replaces “if” for an unproven outcome. | Compare plausible unfavorable and favorable cases and identify decisive assumptions. Enthusiasm is not proof of bias, and adverse outcomes should not be inflated to compensate. |
| Authority bias | A reputation, company practice or framework name does the work of an argument. | Check actual source, context, expertise and mechanism of transfer. Domain authority can be valuable evidence, but does not establish fit to this case or erase disagreement. |
| Status quo bias | Risks of changing are explicit while staying put is treated as safe/default. | Compare action and inaction over the same horizon and criteria, including delay and switching costs. Do not assume change is better simply because inertia is possible. |

## Inference patterns

### Narrative fallacy
A clean causal story may omit confounders, selection, feedback and competing causes. Look for a leap from a sequence or correlation to “because.” Ask what would distinguish the preferred mechanism from the best alternative explanation. A persuasive retrospective account is not a prospective prediction. Preserve an explicitly hypothetical mechanism rather than announcing it as established.

### Conjunction and dependency errors
A plan can require several specific conditions simultaneously while discussing each as though the combination is assured. A conjunction cannot be more likely than any constituent event. If defensible conditional probabilities exist, calculate with the appropriate dependencies; do not multiply invented percentages or assume independence. More conditions alone do not establish that one plan is less likely than another with different conditions. Without reliable probabilities, map necessary dependencies and identify the fragile or unobservable ones.

### Base-rate neglect
Inspect claims that uniqueness alone overrides relevant reference-class outcomes. A good outside view uses a comparable population, clear denominator, selection process and horizon. Then ask which evidenced case-specific differences justify departing from it. A mismatched base rate can be worse than none; mark unavailable or weak reference classes rather than borrowing attractive statistics.

## Challenger and verifier biases

| Pattern | Detection | Correction |
|---|---|---|
| Evidence-convenience confirmation | Most findings concern the best-documented dimension rather than the most consequential one. | Compare attention against the user's actual dependencies. Carry hard-to-research material gaps rather than inventing evidence for them. |
| Generic critique pattern matching | “Execution risk,” “competition,” or “regulation” appears without a case-specific mechanism. | Require a relevant condition, evidence/conditional status and consequence. Specify or remove the finding. A direct textual inconsistency can be specific even without web evidence. |
| Adversarial bias | The role seems required to find failure; supported strengths disappear; neutral gaps become negative facts. | Test the best case for the plan and the possibility the critique is wrong. Permit no material concern and a sound-plan verdict. |
| Confidence inflation | Strong labels rely on indirect sources, fluent wording, unsupported scope or imagined expert agreement. | Narrow to what is supported and explain residual uncertainty. No confidence label follows automatically from source tier. |
| Vivid-example bias | Colorful anecdotes outweigh less memorable patterns with stronger relevance. | Weigh consequence, applicability and evidence quality. Do not substitute excitement for decision value. |
| Repetition-as-corroboration | Several lenses or syndicated articles repeat the same origin/premise. | Record common origins and collapse duplicate inference. Breadth of consequences can justify priority, but repeated interpretation does not strengthen truth. |
| Reflexive downgrading | The verifier changes good findings simply to appear rigorous. | Keep justified claims unchanged; there is no removal quota. Conversely, unchanged findings do not prove a genuine quality pass occurred. |
| Formality/coverage bias | Structured public material looks more credible than relevant messy internal experience; a filled template is treated as completeness. | Check source-to-claim relevance, missing perspectives and limits. A structural pass is not evidence sufficiency. |

## Reporting a bias finding

Use a concrete form: “The supplied draft cites only favorable pilot comments (U3); the supplied negative interview in S4 is not addressed. This may indicate selective evidence use, although the draft may simply be abbreviated. Include that interview in the demand test (F2).”

Avoid “You have confirmation bias” without evidence. Say what the report did to compensate and which question remains. Where the pattern is unsubstantiated, remove it or identify it as an analysis limitation. Never invent numeric gaps, psychological traits or survey results to make a bias label sound precise.
