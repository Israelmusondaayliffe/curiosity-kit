# Story and production preparation

## Find the dramatic task

Start with what is settled. For a thin idea, establish the audience, duration, delivery use, central character, desired change, resistance, cost of failure and an irreversible choice. Ask the next consequential question rather than issuing a generic questionnaire. If the user supplies answers, use them. If asked to draft with assumptions, label them and complete the draft.

Turn a theme into something an actor can do. "Trust" needs a decision such as handing over the only key while keeping the exit in sight. Name what changes between the first and last image. A mood or genre alone supplies neither conflict nor an ending. Offer genuinely different dramatic approaches only if a decision needs them or alternatives were requested; do not force a multi-agent contest.

For an approved script, preserve the lines, scene order, intended outcome and prohibited changes. Identify exact ambiguities instead of rewriting the premise. If the user is developing the script themselves, offer fragments or beat options when asked; otherwise deliver the complete requested scene.

## Write the scene

Write present-tense action and distinct dialogue. Each beat has a visible starting condition, an action or tactic, an opposing response, and an end condition that affects the next beat. Show who learns what before they react. Give a cut or camera move a dramatic reason. Preserve motivated silence and stillness rather than manufacturing movement for every moment.

For a short film, make the smallest scene carry the idea. Remove an unrelated subplot before compressing every action. Estimate playable duration from speech, movement, reaction and transitions; label it as an estimate until footage exists. If the supplied duration cannot fit, explain which action needs removal or another shot. Do not silently accelerate dialogue.

Produce the scene itself, a compact beat list and the coverage decision. Before spending, identify the cheapest scene or shot that could disprove the production approach. A text-only preparation request ends with its actual film package and unperformed proof steps clearly marked.

## Plan production without making a bureaucracy

Use a project bible and ordered scenes/shots. Give stable identifiers to assets, states, scenes and shots. Keep one copy of each approved descriptor, voice and style constant. The selected formula determines prompt construction. For a large project, organize scenes into sequences with dependencies; do not fabricate dozens of empty records.

Plan the work in this order, allowing editing and sound preparation to proceed beside approved shot generation:

1. Brief and creative/practical decisions, with approval reused or requested where needed.
2. Script, sequences, scene purposes and production risks.
3. Naming, formula, visual rules, owners, resources, schedule and cost envelope.
4. Asset, performance, voice, location, wardrobe and prop-state designs.
5. Asset proof and stress tests before claiming readiness for motion.
6. Geography, blocking and motivated shot coverage.
7. Complete prompt compilation and checks.
8. Authorized scene-by-scene attempts, with exact inputs and observed results.
9. Assembly alongside generation, then cleanup, colour, dialogue, ambience, effects and music.
10. Delivery checks, rights/credits, reproducible source pointers and one useful retrospective.

This is an order of necessary decisions, not a required count of forms. A single-shot repair starts at its affected decision. Each real handoff states the work, current inputs, scope/budget and expected result. The film owner resolves disagreement rather than bouncing the same task between helpers.

For each uncertain shot, record the failure hypothesis, visible acceptance condition, proposed number of attempts and a stop/simplify decision. Carry the user's limit. If none exists, propose a finite limit for approval before paid work; a historical 10-to-15-attempt practice is not automatic spending permission. Estimate cost from the chosen service's actual pricing only when available; otherwise leave cost unknown and block spending. Do not invent a shoot date or provider turnaround.

## Ready to proceed

The package needs a readable scene, usable shotlist, asset/geography/performance decisions, complete prompts, and a short preparation note covering unresolved proof, schedule/budget and delivery needs. Approval of planning text does not mean an image has passed a stress test. Name exactly which downstream step waits on media evidence.
